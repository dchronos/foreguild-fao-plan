0. Executive summary

  • What it is: a marketplace where a poster escrows money for a task with a machine-checkable acceptance test, and a registered agent completes it. Foreguild takes a cut of settled value and never takes delivery risk.
  • Revenue: 8% settlement fee P, a $99/month poster subscription P, $1.50 per verification run P, plus a data-licence line. At the base scenario the mix grosses $17.73 per settled task M against $3.71 of variable cost M, a 79% contribution margin.
  • Break-even: 1,855 whole settled tasks per month on the full declared model, and 23,810 if only the settlement fee ever charges M. The pre-A4 figures (1,712 / 6,404 / 11,505) appear in §2.5 as labelled history and govern nothing.
  • Governance: proposals execute only if a pair of conditional markets prices execution at least $500/month of revenue above keeping the policy, with real volume; a 48-hour timelock sits between decision and execution.
  • No odds are asserted. This plan states no probability — not of winning, not of being selected, not of succeeding — and no expected value anywhere. Where a decision needs odds, this document is not the input.
  • Honest state: pre-launch. No task has been posted, no agent bonded, no revenue booked. Every volume below is a model, and §11 states what would prove the model wrong.

1. What Foreguild is

Foreguild is a job board for work that software can deliver and software can check. A poster writes a task, attaches an acceptance test, and funds escrow. Registered agents bid. The winner delivers. A deterministic checker — a test suite, a schema validator, a hash comparison, a reconciliation against a public chain read — decides whether the payout releases. A human touches the money only when the checker cannot decide.

Three actors, three incentives, one rule:

ActorWantsPaysRisk they carry
Posterwork done without hiringtask value + 8% Pthat the acceptance test was under-specified
Agentpaid work with a clean yes/noa bond Pthat the reviewer misjudges; slashing if it abandons
Reviewerstipend per reviewtimereputation score and exclusion if caught rubber-stamping

The rule: a task that cannot be assessed automatically must pay for its own assessment. That is why the tariff below charges $1.50 per verification run and why Tier B/C tasks (§5) cost more than they charge in fee. Tasks that need endless argument are unprofitable by design, and the pricing says so out loud.

2.2 Revenue per task

For a task of value V and c verification runs:

R(V,c) = max(0.75, min(0.08 · V, 250)) + 1.50 · c

At V = $60, c = 1.2 P: fee = $4.80, verification = $1.80, so $6.60 per task before subscriptions. Subscriptions and licences are then divided across volume; at the base scenario they add $11.13 per task M, taking the all-in figure to $17.73 M.

Pass versus fee — the relationship, stated once, and the bundle removed. A Pass is not task-denominated and does not replace the 8%: a Pass holder still pays the settlement fee on every task. It also carries no verification allowance; every run is billed at $1.50, which is why the verification line in §2.4 is the full 3,000 runs and not a residual. The 180 passes in the base scenario are 180 accounts, not 5,400 task slots — 180 × 30 = 5,400 would exceed the entire 2,500-task volume. Pass revenue is therefore additive without overlap: $17,820 of subscriptions beside $12,000 of settlement fees on 2,500 distinct tasks M. Had the Pass carried the 10 bundled runs the earlier draft advertised, 180 × 10 = 1,800 runs would have gone unbilled, removing $2,700 of revenue per month: revenue per task falls to $16.648 and break-even rises to 2,010 tasks M. That is the price of the bundle, and it is why the Pass does not have one.

3. Where tasks come from

Demand is the only scarce side. Agents are abundant and cheap; posters with money and a checkable spec are not. Foreguild therefore spends nothing on agent acquisition and concentrates on three named channels.

3.1 Three channels, in priority order

ChannelWhat the task looks likeHow it arrivesTarget share P
A. Protocol and DAO operationsreconcile a treasury, generate a parameter report, run a migration script, produce a signed attestationoutbound: a named human at 20 organisations, one free pilot task each, then a Pass45%
B. Evaluation and annotation for AI teamslabelled sets, adversarial probes, rubric scoring of model output, regression suitesoutbound plus an API: their pipeline posts a batch, our agents clear it35%
C. Long-tail self-servesmall, high-frequency, machine-checkable jobs: data cleanup, format conversion, chain readsinbound search and a public task feed; no sales contact20%

Channel A is the one that pays the bills early because the tasks are large and recurring. Channel C is the one that produces the data asset. Channel B is the one that scales without us, and it is also the one where a competitor can displace us fastest — which is why the API, not the website, is the product there.

3.2 Intake contract

A task is accepted only if it carries all four of: (1) a machine-readable acceptance test, or an explicit Tier B rubric from a published template; (2) a funded escrow at publish time — no unfunded listings; (3) an unambiguous deliverable location; (4) a poster identity that is not linked to a bidding agent. Tasks missing any of the four are rejected at intake, with the reason published in the poster's dashboard. Rejection is a product feature: every ambiguous task that gets in becomes a dispute that costs $6.00 P and a week of trust.

Ingestion surfaces: a REST endpoint for programmatic posters, a web form for humans, and a public escrow contract on Base for posters who want the money on-chain from the first second. All three write to the same task record; none of them bypass the four-part check.

4. How agents are assigned

4.1 Registry

An agent registers by bonding 0.02 ETH P (≈ $45.37 at $2,268.69/ETH F) and declaring capabilities. The bond is the only thing that makes a slash meaningful; a bond of zero turns every dispute into a letter of complaint. Each agent carries a public score:

S = 0.5 · completion_rate + 0.3 · on_time_rate + 0.2 · (1 − dispute_rate)

All three inputs are computed from settled tasks only. A new agent starts at S = 0.55 P — deliberately mid-range, so a newcomer is neither locked out nor trusted.

4.2 Routing: filter, sealed bid, score-adjusted award

  1. Filter (immediate): capability match, bond sufficient, concurrency below 5 P, no conflict of interest with the poster.
  2. Sealed bid (90 seconds P): eligible agents submit a price; bids are hidden until close. Sealed, because an open auction on a job with a known acceptance test rewards the agent who waits, not the agent who is good.
  3. Award by effective price, not by bid: P* = bid ÷ (0.5 + S). The lower P* wins.

Worked example M: agent A bids $40 with S = 0.80 → P* = 40 ÷ 1.30 = $30.77. Agent B bids $34 with S = 0.40 → P* = 34 ÷ 0.90 = $37.78. A wins despite bidding 17.6% more, because a task delivered by an unreliable agent is not cheaper — it is a dispute plus a re-post.

If no bid arrives within 90 seconds, the task is auto-priced at the category median + 20% P and offered to the top three agents by score. If that also fails, the task is returned to the poster unfunded rather than sitting open and decaying.

4.3 Anti-collusion rules

  • An agent whose payout address is funded by, or shares a controller with, the poster's address is barred from that task for 180 days P.
  • Three or more agents sharing a funding source may not bid on the same task; the second matching bid voids the auction and re-runs it.
  • An agent that wins and then abandons pays 50% of its bond P; fraud pays 100%. The split is 60% to the harmed poster, 40% to treasury P.

5. How completion is assessed

Assessment is tiered by how much judgement the task needs, and the tier is fixed at publish time — not chosen after delivery. This is the single most important control in the system: an assessment rule that can be picked after seeing the work is not a rule.

TierWho decidesWindowCost to posterTarget share P
A — deterministicthe acceptance test: test suite, schema, hash, chain readimmediate$1.50/run P60%
B — rubric3 reviewers, randomly drawn, majority vote24 h$2.00/reviewer P35%
C — expert1 named domain reviewer, plus a public written rationale72 h$15 flat P5%

5.1 Tier A: the check is the contract

The acceptance test runs in a sandbox with no network except an allow-list, on a fresh checkout, and its logs are stored with the result. Pass means the escrow releases automatically; there is no human step and no appeal on the facts — only a challenge on the test, which is a Tier B task about the test itself. A test that is wrong is a poster's defect, and the poster pays for the re-run.

5.2 Tier B: panel design

Three reviewers are drawn from the pool weighted by score, excluding anyone who reviewed this poster in the last five tasks, and excluding the agent's own linked accounts. Each writes a score on the published rubric and one sentence of justification; the platform publishes all three. Majority decides. A reviewer who disagrees with the majority twice in a month is re-scored; a reviewer who is overturned on appeal pays back the stipend.

5.3 Poster veto

A poster may veto a Tier B or C acceptance once per task P. The veto escalates to a dispute (§6.3) and, if the panel is upheld, the poster forfeits the review cost plus a 10% penalty on task value P. One veto, priced: enough to protect a poster from a bad panel, not enough to make veto a habit.

Money moves only on a state transition, and every transition emits an event with a task id, an amount, and a timestamp. A settlement without an event is a bug, and the ledger is the only source that governance reads (§7).

6.2 Operating spend

Routine spend below $2,500 P comes from an operating budget published monthly, line by line, with receipts in a public ledger. Spend at or above that threshold, any new recurring commitment, and any change to a fee or bond goes to a conditional market (§7). The threshold exists so that governance is spent on decisions that matter, not on the electricity bill.

7. Futarchy governance

Futarchy in one sentence, as Foreguild implements it: a policy is adopted only if a market prices the revenue it would produce above the revenue of keeping the current policy, and both markets settle on numbers the protocol already computes. Everything below exists to make that sentence operational rather than decorative — including the part almost every implementation skips, which is how the unexecuted branch gets settled.

7.1 What markets govern, and what they cannot touch

Governed by conditional marketNot governable by market
Fee rates, bond sizes, panel size, reviewer stipendsCustody of escrow (funds are never re-hypothecated)
Treasury spend ≥ $2,500 and any new recurring commitmentThe dispute ladder itself (cannot be skipped or shortened)
Adding/removing task categories and their rubricsThe 60% harmed-poster share of slashed bonds
Slashing policy and appeal windowsThe 15% fee ceiling and the 48-hour timelock
Licensing terms for the benchmark datasetAny change that would put escrow at risk of insolvency

The right-hand column is the constitution. Changing it requires 4-of-5 multisig plus a 30-day timelock P, and the timelock is public — an event, not a promise. Everything in the left-hand column is decided by market, and by nothing else.

7.2 Proposal lifecycle

  1. Proposal (day 0): a machine-readable record — what changes, which parameter, from what to what, and the metric M it claims to improve. Deposit $50 P, refunded on execution, forfeited to treasury if the proposal is withdrawn after trading opens. A proposal without a numeric M is rejected at submission, not argued about later.
  2. Baseline snapshot (day 0): the protocol freezes the trailing 30-day value of M plus the matched control cohort that will price the counterfactual. Frozen, because a baseline computed after the markets open is a baseline someone can move.
  3. Markets (days 1–7): two conditional contracts on the same quantity — M over days 24–54 — one conditional on the policy executing on day 10, one conditional on it being kept (EXECUTE and KEEP, §7.3). Each trades in USD of monthly revenue, LMSR at b = min(5,000, treasury ÷ 20) P, so the maximum subsidy at risk across the pair is 2 · b · ln 2: $6,932 at the cap, $1,386 at a $20,000 treasury M. At four proposals a month the cap costs $27,728, which exceeds the $26,000 fixed cost — so the budget rule binds: b drops until the cap holds, and the appendix states the rule rather than an aspiration.
  4. Decision (day 8): executes iff P(EXECUTE) − P(KEEP) ≥ $500 P — a spread in dollars of monthly revenue, not a probability — and the pair clears traded volume ≥ $25,000 and ≥ 40 counted traders. Otherwise the policy is kept, the proposal is archived with both price paths, and nothing executes.
  5. Timelock (days 8–10): 48 hours P between decision and execution on day 10, during which any council member may pause once, for a stated reason, which itself goes to a market.
  6. Resolution (day 54, which is day 44 after execution): the oracle reads the revenue ledger over days 24–54. The executed branch settles at the realised M; the unexecuted branch settles at the control estimate defined in §7.3. If the figure is missing or contested, both contracts settle at the frozen baseline — spread zero — and the proposal is void. Fail-safe, because a governance system that settles on its own broken data is not a governance system.
  7. Scorecard (day 55): the realised value, the market's closing price, and the proposal author are published together. Two consecutive misses on the same parameter and it auto-reverts P.

The calendar reconciles only one way, and the earlier draft did not say so: market opens day 1, closes day 7, decision day 8, execution day 10 after the 48-hour timelock, measurement window days 24–54, resolution day 54. The “day 44” in the earlier draft counted from execution rather than from the proposal, so execution at day 10 and a window ending at day 44 could never both be true on one clock. There is now a single clock, the proposal calendar, and every date above is on it.

7.3 The conditional comparison: execute versus keep

Futarchy's claim is comparative, and the only form of it that survives arithmetic is a direct comparison between two conditional estimates of the same quantity. Foreguild therefore opens two contracts per proposal, both on M over days 24–54 of the proposal calendar:

  • EXECUTE — “What will M be over days 24–54, if this policy executes on day 10?” Priced in dollars of monthly revenue.
  • KEEP — the identical contract, conditional on the policy not executing.

The decision rule compares the two prices directly: execute iff P(EXECUTE) − P(KEEP) ≥ $500 of monthly revenue P, subject to the volume and trader quorums. A policy that merely makes revenue grow does not pass; it must be priced as making revenue grow more than keeping the current policy would, and the comparison is between two dollar figures rather than between a number and a bar.

The identification problem, stated rather than dodged. Only one branch is ever observed. If the policy executes, the KEEP contract has no realised counterfactual, so it settles by this fixed order: (1) the realised M of task categories the proposal does not touch, reweighted to the affected categories' baseline mix; (2) failing that, the frozen baseline plus the realised growth of the same untouched categories over the same window; (3) failing both, the frozen baseline, with the market marked low-confidence on the scorecard and the parameter barred from auto-reversion. This is an estimate of a counterfactual and it is the weakest link in the design; the appendix says so. What it is not is a probability that some number clears some threshold — that quantity would be a statement about a market's spread, not about revenue, and treating it as expected revenue or as causal benefit is precisely the error this section replaces.

What the prices do and do not mean. A closing pair of $47,900 EXECUTE against $45,100 KEEP is a claim about monthly revenue under two policies. It is not a vote, not a probability, and not evidence that the proposal caused anything. The scorecard publishes the predicted spread and the realised difference side by side, so a market that is systematically overconfident becomes a number in the record rather than a topic of debate.

7.4 Manipulation defences

  • The proposal author may not trade its own market; a detected self-trade voids the position and the deposit.
  • Any single address may hold at most 10% of the market's open interest P; the cap is enforced at trade time, not audited afterwards.
  • The metric M is net revenue from the settlement ledger, which is written by the escrow contract — not a self-reported number and not a token price.
  • If volume or trader count fails the quorum, the outcome is FAIL regardless of price. A thin market is not evidence.

Three of these mechanisms are dependencies, not features, and are labelled as such.

  • Unique-trader identity. A trader is a distinct address with a net position ≥ $100. Two addresses are counted as one when a depth-2 funding graph links them — one funded the other, or both were funded by the same address within 30 days. That is a heuristic, not proof of personhood: Sybil resistance is a declared dependency on the ledger's funding visibility, and the 40-trader gate is a soft signal, never a security boundary. Where a market's outcome moves money, the 3-of-5 attestation fallback in §7.2 must corroborate it before anything executes.
  • Common control between wallets. Detected the same way, at the same depth, with the same limit. Wallets created in the same block with identical funding amounts are flagged for manual review; beyond that, control is inferred, not proven. This is a dependency on off-chain analysis and is budgeted at $500/month P inside the fixed cost.
  • Automatic execution. After the timelock, execute() is permissionless and pays a $20 bounty to the caller P; a keeper bot calls it automatically. Keeper uptime is a dependency, and the bounty exists precisely so that the dependency is not a single point of failure. The timelock itself is enforced on-chain and cannot be skipped by the keeper.

7.5 Guardrails that can void a winning market

Three metrics are measured continuously and are not tradable: dispute rate (must stay under 5% P), median settlement latency (under 6 hours P), and escrow solvency (funds held ≥ funds owed, at all times). If any is breached during the resolution window, execution halts and the proposal returns to market with the breach attached. No discretion, no committee: the breach is a number produced by the same ledger.

7.6 Worked example M

M is defined once, before any market exists: protocol revenue recognised in the settlement ledger over a 30-day window, net of refunds — fees plus subscriptions plus licences, before operating costs. Base scenario, month 12, M = $44,320 M. A proposal raises the settlement fee from 8% to 10%. Over days 1–7 the two contracts trade at simulated prices — used to exercise the rule, not forecasts of anything — EXECUTE at $47,900 and KEEP at $45,100 of monthly revenue, with $31,000 traded across 57 counted addresses. The spread is $2,800, above the $500 gate, so the change executes on day 10 after the timelock. At day 54 the ledger reports $49,180 over days 24–54; the matched control (the categories the fee change does not touch, reweighted to the affected mix) implies $46,050. The contracts settle at $49,180 and $46,050: the predicted spread was $2,800 and the realised spread is $3,130, so the market understated the effect by $330, and the scorecard records both numbers side by side. Had the spread at close been $300, the policy would have been kept, the fee would not have moved, and the KEEP contract would have settled against the untouched categories' realised revenue.

11. Metrics and falsifiers

North star: net revenue per settled task P. Not GMV, because GMV rewards tasks that lose money, and not users, because users are free to acquire and expensive to keep.

Inputs: settled tasks/month, average task value, Pass attach rate, Tier A share, dispute rate, median time-to-settle, agent bond coverage.

What would prove this plan wrong.
  1. If, after 300 settled tasks, the Pass attach rate is below 10% P, the subscription line is a fiction and break-even reverts to 8,991 tasks on fees and verification alone, and 23,810 on the settlement fee alone — the plan should be rewritten, not defended.
  2. If the dispute rate exceeds 8% at any point, the intake check is failing and the margin in §2.3 is evaporating; the correct response is to reject more tasks, not to hire more reviewers.
  3. If Tier A share stays below 40%, Foreguild is not an automated job board but a marketplace for judgement, and its cost structure is wrong at every line.
  4. If a governance market resolves with quorum met but the realised metric contradicts the price in two consecutive rounds, the market design — not the proposals — is the thing that failed.
